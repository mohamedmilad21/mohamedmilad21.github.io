I was asked for 100 testers. I got 20.

An internal beta that missed its target by 80 percent, why "advertise it harder" was the wrong fix, and the two numbers that taught me the difference between usage and value.

November 2025 · 6 min read

The brief was simple. We had a new product in internal beta and needed real feedback on it. My target:

We need to reach 100 internal users and gather their feedback to optimise the product. Do what you think is best. The success of this initiative will be measured by the quality of actionable insights you gather. My product lead

I built a feedback form, pushed it into the product updates channel, timed the announcement for the morning so people would see it as they logged in, and followed up on WhatsApp. Then I chased. Then I chased again.

I got twenty responses. I reported it plainly: we only have 20 responders, I tried a lot to advertise this.

Why "advertise harder" was never going to work

I spent that week treating a low number as a distribution problem. If more people saw the message, more people would try the product. That's a reasonable instinct and it was wrong, for a reason I only understood afterwards.

Internal users are not a captive audience. They have their own jobs. Asking a colleague to test something is asking them to spend their working time on your priority, and no amount of channel posting changes that arithmetic. The people who did respond weren't the ones who saw the message most often — they were the ones for whom trying it cost the least.

What I should have done, in order:

  1. Find the moment, not the audience. Instead of a broadcast asking people to go and try a thing, attach the ask to something they already do daily. A form nobody opens is a distribution failure; a form attached to a task they were doing anyway is not.
  2. Ask fewer people for more. Twenty considered responses from people who genuinely used the product beat a hundred shrugs. I was optimising for the count because the count was in the brief.
  3. Renegotiate the target once the mechanism failed. I chased the number for a week before saying "this isn't working". That week was the actual mistake.

And here's the thing that stings slightly: the brief already told me what mattered. The success measure was the quality of actionable insights. The 100 was a proxy. I chased the proxy and nearly missed the goal.

The other number, from a different feature

Around the same period we ran a food rescue feature — restaurants offering unsold orders at half price rather than throwing them away. After three days the numbers looked like this:

MetricResult
Orders offered at half price44 (31 on day one, 8 on day two, 5 on day three)
Accepted by customers3 — a 7% acceptance rate
Actually collected0 — all three were later refused and cancelled

A 7% acceptance rate is a number you could put in a deck. A 0% completion rate is the truth. The customers who said yes changed their minds before collection, which tells you the real problem wasn't price at all.

Look at the supply side too: 31 offers on day one, then 8, then 5. Partner interest collapsed by 84% inside seventy-two hours. Both sides of the marketplace were telling us the same thing, and only one of them was in the headline metric.

What both numbers have in common

In both cases I had a metric that made the thing look more alive than it was. Twenty responses feels like traction until you ask how many were considered. Three acceptances feels like validation until you ask how many were collected.

The habit I took from it: always find the number one step further down the funnel than the one you're being asked for. Not sign-ups but second orders. Not acceptances but completions. Not responses but usable insights. The step you don't measure is usually where the product is failing.

The takeaway

Missing a target is cheap. Missing it quietly, for a week, while chasing the proxy instead of the goal — that's the expensive version. Report the miss early and renegotiate the mechanism.
